A country’s banknotes are a gallery of portraits that reveals whom its
collective memory honors. In Colombia, paper money has moved from the heroes of
independence to the great names of culture, telling the country’s story along
the way.

## The heroes of independence

For much of the twentieth century the dominant faces were those of the
independence struggle. **Simón Bolívar**, the Liberator, and **Francisco de
Paula Santander**, “the Man of the Laws”, appeared again and again on Banco de
la República issues —Bolívar, for example, on the classic 1,000-peso note. To
them was added **Policarpa Salavarrieta**, “La Pola”, heroine and martyr of
independence, one of the few female faces of that stage.

## Science, letters, and art

In time, paper money widened its pantheon to figures of culture and thought:
writers such as **Jorge Isaacs** or **José Asunción Silva**, and scientists and
humanists who gave their names to different denominations.

## The current family of notes

The series Banco de la República put into circulation from 2016 consolidated
that turn toward culture. Among its protagonists are the painter **Débora
Arango** (2,000), the poet **José Asunción Silva** (5,000), the anthropologist
**Virginia Gutiérrez de Pineda** (10,000), the statesman **Alfonso López
Michelsen** (20,000), the Nobel laureate in Literature **Gabriel García
Márquez** (50,000), and former president **Carlos Lleras Restrepo** (100,000).
For the first time, several women starred on complete denominations.

## More than a portrait

Each face comes with landscapes, motifs, and symbols that complete the story:
monuments, fauna, geography, and historical scenes. To read a Colombian
banknote is, in the end, to read a fragment of national identity.
