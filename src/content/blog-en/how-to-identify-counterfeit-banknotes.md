Central banks build numerous hard-to-copy security features into their notes.
Knowing them helps both to avoid a counterfeit in daily life and to judge a
collectible piece more fairly. The golden rule is simple: **feel, look, and
tilt**.

## Watermark

When you hold the note **to the light**, an image appears —often a portrait or
the denomination— formed by variations in paper thickness. It is one of the
oldest and most reliable defenses: it cannot be photocopied or printed, because
it sits *inside* the paper.

## Security thread

A thin band, embedded in the paper, that appears continuous when held to the
light. On modern notes it often carries microtext with the denomination and
sometimes changes color or windows through the surface.

## Raised printing (intaglio)

Run a fingertip over the main texts and portraits: on a genuine note you feel
the **relief** of intaglio printing. Counterfeits from ordinary printing or
photocopying feel flat.

## Microprinting and see-through register

Tiny texts, readable only with a loupe, that look blurred on copies. The
**see-through register** is a motif that matches exactly on both faces when
held to the light.

## Color-shifting inks

Some elements are printed with **optically variable ink (OVI)**: when you tilt
the note they change hue (for example from gold to green). They are very hard
to imitate.

## Polymer banknotes

More and more countries issue **polymer** notes, with a **transparent window**
and integrated holograms. The transparent window is, by itself, a highly
visible security feature and practically impossible to fake with household
means.

## If in doubt

Always compare with a note you know is genuine and, if you suspect a note in
circulation, consult your bank or central bank. This article is a guide and
does not replace an official check. To get used to the terminology, see our
[glossary of numismatics and notaphily](/en/glossary/).
