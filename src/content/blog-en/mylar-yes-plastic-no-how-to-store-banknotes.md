There is a scene that repeats in the collecting world and that hurts to
watch. Someone inherits a parent’s or grandparent’s banknote collection,
stored with all the love in the world in an album of plastic sleeves bought
thirty years ago. They open the album and find the notes yellowed, inks
running, stuck to the sleeve like old decals. When they try to peel one off,
the paper surface stays glued to the plastic. Decades of history, patience,
and investment, ruined not by neglect but precisely by the attempt to protect.

The culprit has a name: **PVC**, polyvinyl chloride, the most common and
cheapest plastic on the market. And the solution also has a name: **Mylar**, an
archival-quality polyester that museums, national libraries, and professional
grading services have used for more than half a century. This article explains,
without unnecessary jargon but without dodging the chemistry when it matters,
why the difference between one material and the other can be the difference
between a collection that survives generations and one that destroys itself in
silence inside a drawer.

## Paper is living (and fragile) matter

To understand why the sleeve material matters so much, it helps to remember
what a banknote is. Traditional paper money is made of cotton fibers —sometimes
mixed with linen— pressed and interlocked. It is an organic, hygroscopic
material (it absorbs and releases humidity from the air) and chemically
sensitive. The intaglio inks that give it relief, the optically variable inks,
the metallized security threads, and the watermarks live together in an object
designed to last a few years in circulation, not decades in a case.

An uncirculated note, with its crisp paper and intact relief, also keeps
something almost intangible: the **original paper sizing**, that stiffness and
sheen that collectors and grading firms prize so highly. All of that —fibers,
inks, sizing— reacts with what surrounds it. With light, with humidity, with
acids in the air and, above all, with the materials that touch it directly for
years. The sleeve is not a neutral accessory: it is the note’s immediate
chemical environment, twenty-four hours a day, for decades.

And here is the trap: to the eye, all transparent sleeves look the same. A
newly bought PVC sleeve looks clean, shiny, flexible, perfect. The damage it
causes is not visible on the first day, or even the first year. It is a slow,
cumulative, and —important— **irreversible** process.

## What PVC actually is and why it destroys notes

Polyvinyl chloride is, in its pure state, a rigid, brittle plastic. To turn it
into those soft, flexible sleeves that bend without breaking, manufacturers add
**plasticizers**: chemical compounds —usually phthalates— that can represent up
to a third of the material’s weight. That is the first problem, and it is
enormous: the plasticizers are not chemically bound to the plastic. Over time
they migrate. They leave the PVC and deposit themselves on whatever is in
contact with it.

On a banknote that migration shows up as a greasy film, sometimes visible as an
oily sheen on the paper. That film softens the inks, partly dissolves the
pigments, and makes the note stick to the sleeve. When the collector finally
notices something odd and tries to take the piece out, the ink —or the surface
layer of the paper itself— stays glued to the plastic. Veteran collectors talk
of notes **“burned by PVC”**, and the phrase is exact: the damage looks like a
chemical burn.

The second problem is still more insidious. PVC is chemically unstable: with
heat, light, and the simple passage of time, the polymer degrades and releases
**hydrochloric acid** as a gas. Yes, the same laboratory acid, in tiny but
constant concentrations, trapped inside a closed sleeve with the note. That
acid attacks the cellulose fibers of the paper, which turn yellow first, then
brown, and brittle at the end. It also attacks the inks and corrodes metallic
elements such as security threads and metallic inks.

There is a third path of damage almost nobody mentions: the effect accelerates
itself. PVC degradation is **autocatalytic**: the acid it releases speeds the
decomposition of the plastic, which in turn releases more acid. That is why a
collection can go ten years without visible symptoms and deteriorate
dramatically in the next five. When the damage becomes obvious, the process has
already been advancing for years and there is no going back: no restorer can
return lost sizing to the paper or replace dissolved ink.

For those of us who collect in the tropics there is an aggravating factor.
Heat and humidity —the normal climate of Colombia’s hot country, from
Barranquilla to Villavicencio by way of Montería and Cali— multiply the speed
of all these reactions. A PVC sleeve that on the Bogotá savanna would take
twenty years to cause visible damage can do it in five on the coast. The enemy
works faster exactly where more collectors live.

## How to recognize PVC before it is too late

The most frustrating part is that PVC sleeves are rarely sold labeled as such.
They are offered as “protective sleeves”, “banknote sleeves”, “paper-money
album”, with no further information. A few practical clues:

- **The smell.** Plasticized PVC has a characteristic sweet “new plastic”
  smell, like a cheap shower curtain or the interior of a car just off the lot.
  That smell is, literally, the plasticizer evaporating. Mylar and
  polypropylene smell of nothing.
- **Excessive flexibility.** PVC sleeves are soft, rubbery; they fold and
  wrinkle easily and spring back. Polyester is stiffer, with a “crisp” feel and
  a crease that leaves a mark.
- **Price and provenance.** Cheap albums with integral pockets, especially old
  and generic unbranded ones, are suspect by default. If the seller cannot say
  what the sleeve is made of, assume the worst.
- **Signs in old sleeves.** Yellowing of the plastic itself, a sticky surface,
  stiffness where there used to be flexibility (a sign the plasticizer has
  already migrated), or that oily patina inside: all indicate PVC in full
  degradation. If an album shows any of these, the notes must come out at once,
  not next week.

It is worth saying clearly: if part of your collection is in sleeves whose
material you do not know, the cost of replacing them with archival sleeves is a
tiny fraction of the value of almost any collectible note. It is probably the
best money spent in the whole hobby.

## What Mylar is and why it is different

“Mylar” is a registered trademark —originally DuPont’s, today Mylar Specialty
Films— for biaxially oriented polyethylene terephthalate film, a polyester
known by its English initials: **BoPET**. In conservation, when people speak of
archival-quality Mylar they usually mean specific grades such as **Melinex
516**, the film used by the Library of Congress, national archives around the
world, and the main banknote grading services to encapsulate pieces.

The difference with PVC is not of degree but of nature:

- **It contains no plasticizers.** Polyester is flexible by its own molecular
  structure, with no additives to migrate. There is no greasy film, no softened
  inks, no notes stuck to the sleeve. What is not in the material cannot leave
  it.
- **It is chemically inert.** BoPET does not react with cellulose, inks, or
  metals under normal storage conditions. It releases neither acids nor gases.
  A note stored in Mylar is, chemically speaking, surrounded by nothing: the
  ideal setting for a paper object.
- **It is stable over time.** While PVC degrades and accelerates its own
  breakdown, archival polyester keeps its properties for decades. The
  accelerated-aging tests required by conservation standards —such as the
  Photographic Activity Test (PAT) and ISO specifications for housing
  materials— it passes with ease.
- **It is a real barrier.** Besides not damaging on its own, polyester resists
  the passage of humidity and atmospheric pollutants well, and its transparency
  and stiffness protect the note physically: against accidental folds, finger
  grease, and rubbing.

An honest nuance: Mylar is not magic. It is an inert material, not an active
one. It does not neutralize acids the note already carries, nor does it undo
previous damage. A note that spent years in PVC and is already contaminated
will still have that residue on the paper even if you change the sleeve (in
those cases, evaluation by a professional conservator is the only sensible
path; never try to “clean” a note yourself). What Mylar guarantees is that,
from the day the note enters the sleeve, the housing material stops being a
threat.

## And the other plastics? The full map

Between the villain (PVC) and the gold standard (archival polyester) there is a
middle zone that causes confusion. A quick survey:

- **Polypropylene and polyethylene.** Both are chemically stable and carry no
  plasticizers, so they are considered safe for contact with paper. They are
  used in inexpensive sleeves of acceptable quality. Their disadvantages versus
  polyester are physical, not chemical: they are softer, scratch easily, protect
  less against handling, and their transparency is inferior. For low-value notes
  or trading duplicates, an additive-free polypropylene sleeve is a reasonable
  option. For the important pieces of the collection, polyester remains the
  answer.
- **“Acetate.”** Some old products use cellulose acetate, which over time
  develops so-called “vinegar syndrome”: it degrades releasing acetic acid
  (hence the vinegar smell) and damages what it holds. It should be avoided
  like PVC.
- **Glassine paper.** The translucent glassine envelopes traditional in
  philately are not plastic, but they deserve a mention: ordinary glassine is
  slightly acidic and is not meant for decades of contact. If used, it should
  be a temporary wrap, never a definitive housing.
- **Enemies that are not sleeves.** The list of avoidable damage does not end
  with plastic: rubber bands (vulcanized rubber releases sulfur and leaves
  permanent dark marks), metal clips (rust), adhesive tape of any kind (the
  worst individual enemy of a note after PVC), hot lamination (total,
  irreversible destruction of collectible value), and “magnetic” albums with
  self-adhesive pages. None of these things should be within a meter of a
  collection.

## In good company: famous documents that live in polyester

If the chemical argument sounds theoretical, the institutional one is
overwhelming: the most valuable paper pieces on the planet are stored in
exactly the material this article recommends.

The Library of Congress pioneered polyester-film encapsulation in the 1970s and
today applies it routinely to its collection of historical maps, nineteenth-
century newspapers, and manuscripts: the document is sealed between two sheets
of inert polyester that allow it to be consulted without ever being touched.
The U.S. National Archives use the same technique for thousands of frequently
consulted documents. And for their absolute jewels —the Declaration of
Independence and the Constitution— they took the principle to the extreme:
sealed chambers filled with argon gas, without a single reactive molecule
around the paper. It is the museum version of the same idea a Mylar sleeve
applies at home: surround the document with nothing.

The British Library protects manuscripts and historical documents with Melinex,
the same archival polyester film mentioned above. And you need not go that far:
in Colombia, the **Archivo de Bogotá** makes its own conservation units —
polyester envelopes and rolls, folders and boxes with “archival quality”
materials— to hold the city’s documentary heritage, following the conservation
guidelines the Archivo General de la Nación sets for the whole country. When
you order polyester sleeves for your notes, you will be using the same
technology with which Bogotá stores its colonial documents.

The private collecting world tells the same story. Copies of *Action Comics #1*
—Superman’s first appearance, the most valuable comic in the world, with sales
in the millions of dollars— are conserved and traded in Mylar sleeves; no
serious comics collector would use anything else. The great dealers of classic
stamps ship their rare pieces in polyester protectors. And the sealed holders
of banknote graders such as **PMG** enclose each piece precisely in inert film:
when you buy a certified note, it is already living in the correct material.

The pattern is impossible to ignore: national libraries, state archives,
auction houses, and graders —institutions that answer for paper valued in
fortunes and for irreplaceable historical memory— all converged on the same
material. Nobody stores a Magna Carta in PVC.

## How to set up a proper housing system

With the diagnosis clear, the treatment is simple and within any budget. A
sensible conservation system has three layers:

1. **First layer: the individual sleeve.** Each note in its own archival
   polyester sleeve (Mylar/Melinex) or, at minimum, additive-free
   polypropylene, of the right size —the sleeve should overlap the note by a
   few millimeters on each side, without squeezing it or letting it rattle.
   Specialized makers of archival material state the material explicitly and
   the “acid-free” and “PVC-free” condition; that transparency in the spec
   sheet is itself a quality signal. For high-value pieces there are rigid
   double-layer polyester portfolios, and of course the professional
   encapsulation of the graders, which uses precisely this kind of sealed inert
   film.
2. **Second layer: organization.** Individual sleeves go in acid-free boxes
   with an alkaline reserve, or in albums whose pages are also archival. Watch
   the classic error: buying excellent sleeves and putting them in an album of
   PVC pages. The acid gases of the album still reach the notes. The
   conservation chain is as strong as its weakest link.
3. **Third layer: the environment.** Not even the best sleeve compensates for a
   bad setting. The ideal conditions are well known: cool temperature and —more
   important than the exact figure— stable, moderate relative humidity around
   45–55%, without sharp swings, and darkness or minimal light, always without
   direct sun or nearby fluorescents. In humid climates, silica-gel packets
   (renewed periodically) inside the storage box help a great deal, as does
   choosing an interior cupboard rather than an exterior wall, and never a
   basement or attic. And a final habit that costs nothing: handle notes by the
   edges, with clean dry hands, on a clear surface.

## Not only banknotes: all collectible paper is at stake

Everything said so far applies, almost word for word, to the rest of the paper
we usually collect alongside banknotes. Anyone who gathers paper money often
also accumulates stamps, old bank documents, checks, stocks and bonds,
postcards, photographs, period newspaper clippings, even tickets and lottery
slips. Each of those objects is cellulose plus ink, and each suffers in PVC
exactly as a banknote does: yellowing, running inks, adhesion to the sleeve.

Some deserve particular warnings. **Stamps** are especially vulnerable because
their original gum —that reverse adhesive that weighs so much in their
valuation— reacts to humidity and plasticizers with alarming ease: a mint stamp
can weld itself to a PVC sleeve in a few years of humid climate. **Old
photographs** add layers of chemical emulsion that PVC gases attack directly;
for them, archival polyester is not a recommendation but a requirement of
photographic conservation standards. Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century
documents present the inverse problem: many were printed on wood-pulp paper
with its own acidity, so besides an inert sleeve they benefit from housings
with an alkaline reserve that buffers their internal deterioration.

Collectors of banking history —checks of vanished banks, bills of exchange,
stock certificates with engraved vignettes— handle pieces that are often
unique: there is no other example waiting in the next auction. In those cases
the economic argument falls short; what is at stake is irrepeatable historical
documentation. The good news is that the solution is the same and is bought in
the same place: polyester sleeves of the right size, acid-free boxes, a stable
environment. A single well-built housing system protects the whole collection,
no matter how many kinds of paper live in it.

## The economic argument, if the chemistry is not enough

If all of the above sounds abstract, the market translates it into figures with
brutality. The price difference between an impeccable uncirculated note and the
same note with yellowing, altered tone, or plastic residue can be several times
its value —and on scarce pieces, figures that hurt. Grading firms note
conservation problems without pity, and terms such as “tone” or “residue” on a
certification label scare serious buyers. PVC damage is not an aesthetic
imperfection: it is a permanent sentence in the piece’s description, auction
after auction.

Do the opposite sum: protecting a hundred notes correctly with archival
polyester sleeves costs less than a single Colombian note of middling interest
at an international auction. There is no other decision in this hobby where so
little money protects so much value.

## An action plan for this weekend

If this article left you looking sideways at your albums, this is the sensible
order of tasks:

1. **Audit.** Review what your collection is stored in. Smell the sleeves,
   judge their flexibility, look for yellowing or sticky surfaces. If in doubt
   about the material, treat the sleeve as suspect.
2. **Rescue.** Take the notes first out of any sleeve with signs of
   degradation, handling them by the edges. If a note resists coming out, never
   force it: stop and consult a conservator or a professional dealer before
   causing greater damage.
3. **Replacement.** Get archival polyester sleeves in the size of your notes
   and transfer the collection, starting with the pieces of greatest historical
   or economic value.
4. **Environment.** Move the collection to a cool, dark place of stable
   humidity, in archival boxes or albums.
5. **Calendar.** Note an annual review. Thirty minutes a year are enough to
   catch any problem in time.

## The sleeve as an act of responsibility

Collecting banknotes is, at bottom, an exercise in custody. The pieces we keep
existed before us and —if we do our work well— will exist after. Every
nineteenth-century Colombian free-banking note, every provisional issue, every
Banco de la República series that survives in collectible grade does so because
a chain of people, over generations, decided to care for it.

Choosing Mylar instead of PVC is not a sophistication of the obsessive
collector: it is the link that falls to us in that chain. PVC is cheap today
and ruinous tomorrow; archival polyester costs a few pesos more today and costs
nothing ever again. Rarely does so small a decision separate so clearly
conserving from destroying.

Your notes cannot choose their sleeve. You can.
