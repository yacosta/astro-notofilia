The history of banking in Puerto Rico is marked by its double colonial
heritage: first Spanish and, from 1898, American. That transition was stamped
on its banknotes and coins, which makes the island especially rich ground for
notaphily.

## Spanish Puerto Rico and its money

Until the late nineteenth century Puerto Rico was an overseas province of
Spain. For generations a mixed metallic currency circulated on the island:
Spanish colonial pieces, worn cobs, and foreign coins that arrived with trade.
The shortage of a stable, trusted circulating medium was a recurring problem
for the island economy and prepared the ground for a formal banking
institution.

## The Banco Español de Puerto Rico

The **Banco Español de Puerto Rico**, established in 1888 by royal provision,
is usually considered the island’s first issuing institution. Besides the
functions of a commercial bank —deposits, loans, and discount— it received the
right to issue notes denominated in **pesos**, finally giving Puerto Rico its
own organized paper money.

## The Puerto Rican peso

In parallel, around 1895 a provincial coin specific to the island was struck,
the **Puerto Rican peso**, with silver fractions. The Banco Español notes and
these provincial coins formed a monetary system distinct from the peninsular
one, designed for local commerce.

## 1898: from the peso to the dollar

The Spanish–American War and the Treaty of Paris of 1898 placed Puerto Rico
under United States sovereignty. One of the most immediate and sensitive
consequences was monetary: the Puerto Rican peso was withdrawn and replaced by
the **U.S. dollar**, in an exchange at an unfavorable rate that meant a loss of
value for many inhabitants of the island.

## From Banco Español to Banco de Puerto Rico

The founding institution itself reflected the change of era: the Banco Español
de Puerto Rico was renamed **Banco de Puerto Rico** in 1913, fully integrated
into the dollar system. Its issues of the last decades of the nineteenth
century and the beginning of the twentieth rank today among the most prized
pieces of Caribbean notaphily.

## For the collector

The notes and coins of this period are tangible testimony of Puerto Rico’s
passage from Spanish colony to U.S. territory. An emblematic example is the
[1-peso exchange note of 1895](/coleccion/puerto-rico/billete-de-canje-1-peso-1895/),
issued by the Ministerio de Ultramar in the last years of Spanish sovereignty.
You can explore more examples in our
