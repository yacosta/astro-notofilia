If you collect Colombian banknotes you have surely noticed it: between 1983 and
2014, Banco de la República notes carry three different imprints. Some read
**“Imprenta de Billetes – Bogotá”**, others **“Imprenta de Billetes – Santa Fe
de Bogotá”**, and the most recent simply **“Banco de la República”**. The Pick
catalog even records them with different abbreviations: IBB and IBSFB. The
question arises on its own: are we looking at two or three different firms?

The answer is that they are **one and the same printing works**, and the
explanation is a small jewel of Colombian history hidden in paper money.

## A press that is not a company

The Imprenta de Billetes is not a private firm hired by the central bank: it is
an **internal department of Banco de la República itself**. It began operations
formally on 23 October 1959, printing as its first job the 1-peso note designed
by the Italian Mario Baiardi. Since then it has operated without interruption,
first in Bogotá’s industrial zone and later in the complex we now know as the
Central de Efectivo. In 1994 a Bank of England report praised its technical
level, comparing it with European printers.

## The city that changed its name (twice)

So why did the imprint change? The key is not the press, but the city where it
works.

The Constitution of 1991 officially renamed the capital **“Santa Fe de
Bogotá”**. The press, faithful to formality, updated its seal: that is why
notes of the 1990s read “Imprenta de Billetes – Santa Fe de Bogotá”. It is the
same reason people born in the capital between 1991 and 2000 are, technically,
*santafereños* and not *bogotanos*.

An example that lets you study that stage closely is the
[Banco de la República 2,000 pesos oro note](/coleccion/colombia/banco-de-la-republica-2000-pesos-oro/),
present in the Notofilia catalog with different dates and printers.

In 2000, Legislative Act 01 restored the city’s traditional name: **Bogotá
D. C.** But instead of reversing the seal, the notes went on to identify simply
the issuing and printing entity: “Banco de la República”. A change of style,
not of factory.

## Before 1959: Colombian notes were printed abroad

The fact makes the history of this press even more valuable. For decades
Colombia depended on foreign printers: the **American Bank Note Company** of
New York printed the first Banco de la República notes in 1923 and the pesos
oro series of the 1930s and 1940s; **Waterlow & Sons** of London produced
early-twentieth-century Government notes; and **Thomas De La Rue** printed
several denominations well into the 1980s.

The 2,000 pesos oro note is a perfect case study: its first dates (1983–1986)
came from De La Rue’s London workshops, and the later ones (1986–1992), with
the same design, from the Imprenta de Billetes in Bogotá. There were also
occasional guests: the German **Bundesdruckerei** printed the commemorative
5,000 pesos of 1986, and the **Banco de México** press the 10,000 pesos of
1992.

## List of printers of Colombian banknotes

For the collector’s quick reference, these are the main companies and presses
that have produced Colombia’s notes, with approximate periods and some
outstanding issues:

| Printer | Pick abbr. | Country | Period and notable notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Imprenta de Billetes of Banco de la República** | IBB / IBSFB | Colombia | 1959–present. First job: 1-peso note (1959). Sole printer from the 1990s; printed the 50,000-peso note of 2000 dedicated to Jorge Isaacs and the entire 2016 family. |
| **American Bank Note Company** | ABNC | U.S. | c. 1880–1950s. Nineteenth-century private-bank notes and Banco Nacional; first Banco de la República notes (1923); pesos oro series of the 1930s and 1940s. |
| **Waterlow & Sons Ltd.** | W&S | England | c. 1900–1920s. Government notes of c. 1904 with portraits of Bolívar and other founding figures. |
| **Thomas De La Rue & Co.** | TDLR | England | 1960s–1980s. Several denominations; emblematic case: the 2,000 pesos oro of 1983–1986 (P-430), whose design later passed to the IBB. |
| **Bundesdruckerei** | BDDK | West Germany | 1986. Commemorative 5,000 pesos for the centenary of the Constitution of 1886, with Rafael Núñez. |
| **Imprenta del Banco de México** | — | Mexico | 1992. Commemorative 10,000 pesos for the 500th anniversary of Columbus’s voyage (P-437, with the Embera woman on the obverse). |
| **National lithographers** (the “Litografía Nacional” and Bogotá workshops) | — | Colombia | Nineteenth century–1902. Regeneración issues and emergency notes of the Thousand Days’ War. |

An honest caveat: in the nineteenth century dozens of private banks issued their
own paper, and some individual notes are attributed in catalogs to other New
York firms of the period —Homer Lee Bank Note Co., National Bank Note Co.,
among others. Those attributions are checked note by note in the *Specialized
Issues* volume of the Pick catalog.

## What this means for the collector

For cataloguing purposes, IBB and IBSFB are not two printers but two seal
variants of the same institution, and that distinction creates very concrete
collectible varieties: the same design can exist with two or even three
imprints according to date. Checking that detail in the fine print of the note
is one of those satisfactions only a notaphilist understands: in a space of
millimeters sit the Constitution of 1991, a legislative act of 2000, and more
than sixty years of monetary sovereignty printed at home.
