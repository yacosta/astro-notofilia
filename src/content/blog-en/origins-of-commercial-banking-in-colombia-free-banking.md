For much of the nineteenth century Colombia had neither a central bank nor a
single currency issued by the state. In its place ran a little-known system
that fascinates collectors: **free banking**, an era when private banks printed
and put their own notes into circulation.

## The context: liberal federalism

The starting point was the Constitution of Rionegro of 1863, which shaped the
**United States of Colombia**, a federal republic of marked liberal character.
Under its philosophy of minimal economic intervention, founding a bank and
issuing notes was, in practice, a private activity open to anyone who could
raise the capital. There was no state monopoly of issue.

## The first banks

**Banco de Bogotá**, founded in 1870, is usually named as the country’s first
modern commercial bank and opened the way for a whole generation of
institutions. In the following years banks appeared in the main commercial
plazas —Bogotá, Medellín, Barranquilla, Cartagena, and others— each with the
right to issue its own notes, backed and convertible into gold or silver on
demand of the bearer.

## How those notes worked

Each bank designed and printed its notes, often commissioning the engraving
from lithographic houses in Europe or the United States. The result was an
enormous variety of designs, denominations, signatures, and vignettes:
portraits of founding figures, allegories, commercial scenes. That diversity,
together with limited print runs and the fragility of the paper of the time,
explains why free-banking issues are so sought after in notaphily today.

## The end: the Regeneración

The system began to close with the creation of **Banco Nacional** in 1880,
during the political rise of Rafael Núñez. With the **Regeneración** and the
centralist Constitution of 1886, the state took control of issue: Banco
Nacional notes received forced legal tender (*curso forzoso*) and the private
banks’ right to issue money was progressively restricted. By about 1886, the
era of free banking had come to an end.

## Legacy for the collector

Those notes are key pieces of Colombian economic history and of any serious
collection of the country’s paper money. A good example is the
[50-centavos note of Banco de Medellín](/coleccion/colombia/banco-de-medellin-50-centavos/),

nineteenth-century designs.
